# The website brief

This is the build brief for every client website from Local Cascade. **You are building the
website yourself, directly, in `public/`.** Everything you will ever know about this client comes
from `site.json`, what the operator gives you in the chat, and your own research, so the research
below is the job.

"The first build" means the design system, the header, the footer, the mobile buttons and the
whole home page, finished, before any other page exists. Every later page reuses them unchanged.

Where this brief and `CLAUDE.md` disagree about how to work (branches, shipping, files), follow
`CLAUDE.md`. Where they disagree about what the site says or looks like, follow this brief.

Read this whole brief before you start. Everything we already have on the client is in
`site.json`, which follows the CLIENT INFORMATION list directly below. Some of it will be blank.
Anything blank is yours to go and find, and anything you still cannot find is marked [VERIFY] on
the page.

FIRST, RESEARCH THE BUSINESS PROPERLY
This is most of the work, and the whole website depends on it. Do not skip it and do not come
back with questions you could have answered by reading their website.
- Open their current website and read every single page. Services, prices, staff, qualifications,
hours, locations, the words they use about themselves, and the list of pages they have. Check
their sitemap as well, usually their address with /sitemap.xml on the end, because pages that
are not in the menu still rank in Google.
- Read their Facebook page, their Instagram and their Google Business Profile. Take the address,
phone number, hours, services, photographs, and anything they say about the business.
- Read their Google reviews. You may quote a real review. You may never write one.
- Search for the business and the owner by name. Real, checkable credentials only: qualifications,
where they trained, associations, roles with local teams or organisations, press.
- Work out which towns and areas they actually serve.
Everything the CLIENT INFORMATION list left blank, you fill in from that research.
THEN WORK OUT THE NICHE, AND WRITE FOR IT
This brief is written for any kind of business. The site you build is not. It
is written for this one business in its own trade, and a reader in that trade should never be
able to tell they started from a general brief.
From your research, write down for yourself before you plan anything:
- Exactly what kind of business this is. Not "a health business" or "a service company", but "a
family chiropractic office", "a med spa", "a residential roofer", "a personal injury law firm".
- What that trade calls the people it serves (patients, clients, homeowners, members, guests), and
use that word everywhere. Wherever this brief says "customer", you write the trade's word.
- The words, services, credentials, licences and associations that matter in that trade, and the
questions and worries somebody has before they hire one.
- The advertising and claims rules that apply to that trade, for example health claims for a
clinic, state bar rules for a law firm, licensing and insurance wording for a contractor.
- What the best websites in that trade do well, and what the weak ones all get wrong.
Every section below is then filled in with that knowledge. Where this brief says "the business",
the site uses the real name and the real trade. Where it gives a general rule, you turn it into a
specific rule for this trade. Nothing generic goes on the site.
THE STANDARD WE ARE AIMING AT
Open these websites on a desktop and on a phone and study them before you plan anything.
- https://washingtonpachiro.com/ shows how complete a home page has to be, how many ways to get
in touch there are, and where the call and contact buttons sit.
- https://www.sparksfamilychiropractic.com/ shows the look we want for a local business. Real
photography, confident type, clear sections and nothing that looks like a template.
- https://divine-home-care-2.vibepreview.app/ and https://freedom-home-care.vibepreview.app/ are
two home care sites at the standard we want. The new site must be at least as good as all four
reference sites, and ideally better.
- https://www.apple.com/ shows the level of polish. Generous white space, big clean type, one idea
per section, and the way the page moves as you scroll.
The first two are chiropractic offices. Take their structure, their standard and their feel, never
their words and never their trade specifics, unless this client is in the same trade. Before the
first build, write down for yourself what to take from each one in concrete terms (sizes, spacing,
layout, movement, where buttons sit), and build to that.
WHEN SOMETHING IS STILL MISSING
Never invent a fact. No price, no credential, no statistic, no review, no customer count, no
star rating.
- If a fact is missing and the page needs it, write the literal word [VERIFY] where it should go,
on the page. It then shows up on the finished website, we spot it, and we ask the client for it.
- If a photograph or a video is missing, leave a clearly labelled placeholder
(`data-placeholder="..."`) saying exactly what shot is needed. Never substitute a stock photo.
- Do not send the blanks back as questions. Research them, or mark them.
WHAT THE SITE HAS TO COVER
Build all of the following, in your own words, filled in with what you found.
1. THE BUSINESS
What the business is, the exact trade it is in, who the owner is, what they trained in or are
licensed for, how long they have been going, and the full list of what they offer. Facts only, from your research.
2. THE STRATEGY
This is the business's flagship website. Two kinds of people land on it and it has to work for
both.
The first has never heard of them. They searched Google, or Google Maps, or asked an AI assistant
for somebody near them who does what this business does, and this site came back as one of the
answers. It has to tell them what the business does, where it is, what it costs and why it is any
good, fast enough that they do not go back and click the next result instead.
The second already knows the name. They were referred, or they saw an ad, or they have been putting
it off for a month. They are here to read more, decide whether these are the right people, and
build enough trust to do something about it.
Both of them act in one of only three ways. They call, they fill in the form, or they open the
chat widget. Every page is written to get one of those three to happen.
It has to rank, in Google and inside AI assistants when somebody asks one for a business like this
in this town. Everything in section 10 exists for that.
It has to show the services and the prices plainly, and it has to move the reader towards doing
something about it before they leave the page.
Then the part that is specific to this client. What this site has to do differently from the one
it replaces. Work it out from their current
website. Which single offer or service earns the home page hero, and which one sits second and
never in the hero. If their current site sends everybody to the cheapest thing they sell, say so
and fix it. If there is nothing wrong beyond it being dated and hard to use, say that plainly and
rebuild around one clear enquiry path.
3. WHO THE SITE IS FOR
Age range, the towns they live in, the state they are in when they search, what they have already
tried, and what they want to know before they pick up the phone. Write at a 5th to 8th grade reading
level.
4. THE EXACT DETAILS, USED EVERYWHERE
The business name, legal name, address, phone, email, hours day by day, review link and social
profiles, written out once and used identically on every page. These values are never
paraphrased and never abbreviated differently in two places.
5. BRAND AND DESIGN
The brand colours by hex value, and the rules for using them: which colour fills a button, which
colour the text on that button is, which colour a full-width band is and that it always carries
white text. Check the accent colour's contrast as text on white and, if it fails, never
use it as text. The brand font, or Montserrat if none was given. Then one sentence on
what the site should feel like and one on what it must not. In the first build these values become
the design system described under THE FIRST BUILD DECIDES WHETHER THE WEBSITE IS ANY GOOD, with
sizes and roles, never as adjectives.
Movement. The site should flow as somebody scrolls it, the way Apple's product pages do. Sections
settle into place as they come into view, headings and images ease in rather than all landing at
once, and everything that moves does so smoothly and quickly. Expensive and calm, never busy.
Nothing bounces, nothing spins, nothing slides in from off screen for the sake of it, and no
animation ever delays somebody reading a line or pressing a button. If a visitor has asked their
device for reduced motion, turn all of it off.
6. THE CLIENT'S OWN FILES
Every image from the CLIENT INFORMATION list (`site.json` `images`), used as given, with what
each one is and where it may and may not appear. Never recreate, redraw, recolour or restyle the
logo.
7. PHOTOGRAPHY
Say exactly which photographs exist and which do not. For every shot that does not exist, a
labelled placeholder naming the shot needed. If an image can only be used cropped, say how.
8. THE PAGES
Build a page list and build those pages and no others. About 20 pages, and
these ceilings are hard: five service pages at the most, six town pages at the most. If the
business sells more than five things, the five biggest get pages and the rest get a paragraph.
Every page the client's current website has must have a page on the new site that covers the same
ground, so nothing they currently rank for disappears. Include a contact page, a page for the
owner, a first visit or what to expect page, a pricing page, a reviews page, a service areas page, an FAQ page, and
a plain facts page for AI answer engines. No blog and no blog posts.
9. A BRIEF FOR EVERY PAGE
The longest part, and the part that decides whether the site is any good. For each page: its
sections in order, what each section says, the exact main heading, the copy that has to be close
to word for word, the buttons and where each one goes, then the page title and the description.
Never write "add a section about X". Say what the section is, what it says, and what the reader
does next.
10. SEARCH ENGINES AND AI ANSWER ENGINES
The site has to be built to rank, in Google and inside AI assistants, and carry everything that
needs. All of this, filled in from your research so nobody ever has to replace a value:
- A different page title under 60 characters and a different description of 140 to 158 characters
on every page, each naming the subject, the town and the phone number.
- One main heading per page, then headings in order with none skipped.
- Under the main heading, a direct answer of 40 to 60 words in plain language, before any selling,
written so it still makes sense quoted on its own.
- Key facts, each one a complete standalone statement, placed inside the sections they belong to
as well designed cards (the price in the pricing section, the session length in the first visit
section, the towns in the areas section). Never gather them into one summary box, and never a
section or box called "At a glance", "Quick facts", "Key facts", "Fast facts" or anything like
it. AI site builders add one of these by habit. Do not, on any page.
- Sub-headings written as the actual questions a customer would type or say, not labels. "How much
does it cost in this town" and never "Pricing".
- Full structured data in the site-wide head describing the business: type, name, legal name,
description, web address, logo, phone, email, the year they started, the full address, the map
coordinates, opening hours for each day, the areas served, and links to their social profiles.
Plus structured data for the FAQ. Leave the star rating and the review count out of it entirely
unless a real, checkable number exists.
- Open Graph tags and a canonical address on every page.
- Descriptive alt text on every image, and descriptive link text, so "read how this works" and
never "click here".
- Short lower case web addresses with hyphens.
- No tables anywhere on the site, of any kind. Prices, comparisons, hours and anything else that
looks like rows and columns are shown as cards, a list of price cards, or label and value pairs
in real text, never as a picture of text.
- The business name, what it is and the town it is in, written into the first paragraph of the
home page, the owner's page and every service page, so an AI assistant can lift it out.
- Facts written so they survive being quoted alone, so "a session at this business in this town
takes 15 to 20 minutes" and never "sessions take 15 to 20 minutes".
- Every trade or technical term defined in one plain sentence the first time it is used.
- On a town page, the town's name in the main heading, the first paragraph, at least one
sub-heading and the description.
- An XML sitemap, and robots settings that allow GPTBot, OAI-SearchBot, ChatGPT-User, ClaudeBot,
Claude-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, Google-Extended and Bingbot.
- The whole site readable with images and scripts switched off, so no heading, no body copy and no
contact detail exists only inside a picture or only after a script runs.
11. TERMS AND PRIVACY POLICY
Read the terms and the privacy policy on the client's current website and reproduce them on the
new site word for word, updated only where a detail is plainly wrong or out of date. If their
current site has neither, write both properly for this business, and include in the privacy policy
that the business may contact people by text message about their enquiry and how somebody stops
those messages.
12. THE THREE THINGS THAT HAVE TO BE ON THE SITE
These come from the CLIENT INFORMATION list (`site.json` `integrations`): your CRM's form embed,
chat widget and review link (for example from GoHighLevel). Use them exactly as they are given,
character for character.
- The website form embed code. Wherever a form belongs on the site, it is this form. Full width of
the content column, at least 600px tall. Build no form fields of your own. The form's own script
loads once per page, not once per form.
- The chat widget embed code. Once, site-wide, in the footer. Build no chat of your own. On a phone the widget button sits in the bottom right corner, in line with the call and
contact buttons described in section 17.
- The review link. This is where every "leave us a review" link on the site points. A review link
is all that goes on the site, and nothing on the site explains how review requests work.
13. COPY RULES, HARD
- No em dashes and no en dashes anywhere.
- No colons inside headings or inside body sentences. Vertical bars are allowed in page titles
only, never in body copy.
- Never "it is not X, it is Y" or any version of it. No metaphors, no drama, no invented names for
concepts.
- Numbers as digits. 12, not twelve. US English and US dates.
- Short sentences. The fact, then the number, then what to do about it. The reader is "you", the
business is "we".
- Never use: miracle, cure, guaranteed, painless, revolutionary, secret, breakthrough, unlock,
dominate, skyrocket, transform your life.
14. CLAIMS DISCIPLINE
For every business:
- Never promise or guarantee a result, an outcome, a timeline or a saving.
- Any before and after picture is captioned as a real customer image used with permission, or
left out.
- The contact form asks only for what an enquiry needs, and never for anything sensitive.
- Apply the claims and advertising rules you found for this trade as hard rules.
If the business is in healthcare (chiropractic, physical therapy, medical, dental, wellness), add
all of these as well:
- Never state or imply that any treatment cures, reverses or is guaranteed to fix any condition.
Never use the words reverse or reversal about nerve damage or neuropathy.
- Never mention cancer anywhere on the site.
- Never promise a recovery time, or that surgery will be avoided. What is allowed: the care is
non-surgical and drug free (where that is true), patients often report improvement, results vary
from person to person.
- Any x-ray or scan is captioned as a real patient image used with permission, or left out.
- The contact form never asks anybody about their health.
15. NO BOOKING CALENDARS AND NO PAYMENTS, ABSOLUTE
The site captures an enquiry and hands it to the business. It never books a time and it never
takes money.
- No booking calendar, scheduling widget, date or time picker, availability grid or calendar
embed, and no space reserved for one.
- Every button saying Book, Schedule or Request an Appointment goes to the form or to the phone
number. Nothing else.
- No checkout, cart, order form, payment field or card field, and no page whose job is finishing a
purchase.
- Prices are published so somebody knows the cost before calling. They are never paid on the site.
Never "Buy now", "Pay now", "Add to cart", "Claim your spot", "Reserve your time" or "Checkout".
16. ON EVERY PAGE, WITHOUT EXCEPTION
- No breadcrumbs and no page path anywhere on any page. Never "Home > Services", "Home / About"
or any trail showing where the page sits in the site, not above the heading, not in the hero and
not in small text. The header menu is the only navigation.
- The tappable phone number in the header and in the footer.
- At least one form on the page, and at least one link that opens a text message.
- A closing call to action with the form, then a final one offering phone, text and form.
- Never more than about 1000px of scrolling with no way to make contact.
- On a phone, the two circular buttons from section 17 are on screen the whole way down.
- If the business is in healthcare, on a page about a condition or a treatment, a closing line
saying the page is general information and not medical advice, that individual results vary, and
to ask about your own case. Plus a "medically reviewed by" line naming the owner and linking to
their page, plus a visible last updated date. If the trade has its own required disclaimer (a
law firm's attorney advertising notice, a contractor's licence number), it goes on every page it
applies to instead.
17. NAVIGATION AND FOOTER, THE SAME EVERYWHERE
- Sticky header. Logo on the left. A services menu, then the problems they solve (conditions for a
clinic, if the trade has them), an about menu holding the owner, first visit or what to expect,
and reviews, then pricing, service areas and contact.
- On the right of the sticky header, two things side by side. First the phone number, written out
and tappable, which dials the business when it is pressed. Then a "Contact us" button, which
takes the visitor to the website form.
- On a phone, the sticky header carries the logo and a hamburger button. The phone number and the
"Contact us" button both live inside the menu that the hamburger opens, at the bottom of it,
below the page links. They sit far enough up from the bottom of the screen that the chat widget
button, and the message bubble that pops out of it, never cover either one.
- On a phone, and outside that menu, two circular buttons sit on top of the page at all times,
pinned to the bottom of the screen. A call button in the bottom left corner, which dials the
business. A "Contact us" button in the bottom middle, which goes to the website form. The chat
widget button sits in the bottom right corner, so leave that corner empty for it. The two
buttons look like this:
- Icons only. A phone icon and a message icon, no text on the button, with a hidden label
("Call us", "Contact us") for screen readers.
- No border.
- Exactly the same size as the chat widget button, and lined up with it on the same level, so
the three read as one even row across the bottom of the screen.
- Behind the three buttons, a blurred strip across the bottom of the screen, so the page behind
them goes soft. The strip has no hard top edge. It fades gently and smoothly into the page
above it, so you cannot see where it starts.
Both buttons stay visible however far down the page somebody has scrolled, and there is enough
padding at the bottom of every page that neither the buttons nor the strip ever covers the last
line of content.
- On a phone, every Contact us button, in the header menu, at the bottom of the screen and anywhere
on the page, scrolls so that the website form sits in the middle of the screen. The whole form is
in view, with nothing cut off at the top under the sticky header and nothing cut off at the
bottom behind the buttons.
- A four column footer: the business details and the areas served, the services links, the other
page links, then the hours day by day with a button and a "leave us a review" link. Under the
columns, links to the privacy policy and the terms, the social icons, and the legal business
name.
- Every one of these buttons has to work, and the mobile buttons are the ones AI-built sites break
most. Every call button is a real phone link that opens the dialler with the business number.
Every Contact us button scrolls to or opens the website form, and the form is actually there
when it lands. The two circular mobile buttons sit above every other element on the page, so no
section, image, menu or form ever covers them or swallows the tap, and they work on every page,
not only the home page.
18. BANNED, ABSOLUTE
No countdown timers, discount wheels, popups that block the page, lens flares, dark funnel
styling, fake urgency badges, bought award seals, invented trust badges, auto-scrolling review
carousels, animated gradients, or emoji in headings and buttons. No stock photos of handshakes,
models in scrubs, glowing spine graphics or any other stock cliche of this trade. No newsletter signup. No breadcrumbs or page path such as "Home > Services". No tables. No "At a glance" section or summary fact box. No second phone number. No
address other than the real one. No price that was not found in the research.
THE FIRST BUILD DECIDES WHETHER THE WEBSITE IS ANY GOOD
Everything built after the first build inherits from it. The header, the footer, the colours,
the type, the spacing, the buttons and the home page are all set in the first build, and every
later page copies them. A thin first build produces a template that looks like every other
AI-built site, and no later page can fix that. So the first build gets the most care.
The bar. Put the new home page next to the client's current home page and the new one has to be
plainly the better website at first sight, on a phone and on a desktop. Before the first build,
list for yourself the five things their current site does best, in design and in content, and make
sure the new home page beats every one of them. Then aim past that. The finished site should look
like the business paid a top agency for it.
The first build has to contain all of the following.
1. A DESIGN SYSTEM, SPECIFIED IN NUMBERS, NOT DESCRIBED IN ADJECTIVES
"Modern, clean and professional" means nothing, and the gap gets filled with a template.
Decide values you can build from, and set them up once as site-wide styles in
`public/assets/site.css` that every later page reuses.
- Type. The brand font, or Montserrat. The exact size, weight and line height of the main heading,
section headings, sub-headings, body text, small text and button text, on desktop and on a
phone. Headings tight, body text comfortable, and a small capitals eyebrow label above section
headings.
- Line breaks. Never force a line break inside a heading, a sub-heading or a paragraph. AI-built
sites keep breaking text by hand around the middle of the line and pushing the rest onto a new
line, which leaves half the width empty. Do not do that: text fills the full width of its
container and wraps onto the next line on its own, only when it runs out of room.
A deliberate break is allowed only where the text genuinely looks better split: text beside an
image or another element that narrows it, a centred short statement, or a main heading that
reads better on two lines. Anywhere else, no manual breaks, and no narrow width set on a text
box just to make it wrap early.
- Text width. This is the fix most often needed on AI-built pages, so it is in the design system
and again in the final checklist. Headings, sub-headings and paragraphs
run the full width of the section's content area. No maximum width on a text block, no narrow
centred column and no column split, unless something genuinely sits beside the text, like a
photograph, a form or a card. If nothing is beside it, nothing stops it from flowing the whole
width of the section.
- Colour roles. Which colour is the page background, which is the tinted band, which is the dark
band, and the text colour on each. Which colour fills the main button and the colour of its
text, and the same for the secondary button, links, borders and icons. Where the accent colour
is allowed and where it never appears. Then the rhythm down the page, white, tinted, white,
brand band, white, and never two dark bands in a row.
- Space. The content width, the side padding on a phone, the top and bottom padding of a section
on desktop and on a phone, the gap between columns and cards, the padding inside a card, one
corner radius used everywhere, one soft shadow used everywhere, and the border style.
- Components. The primary button, the secondary button, the text link, the card, the section
header (eyebrow, heading, one line under it), the fact card, the price card, the review card,
the FAQ accordion, the call to action band and the form wrapper. Each one with its own spacing,
colours and hover state. One line-icon set with one stroke width in one colour. Never emoji,
never clip art, never multicoloured icons.
- Call and contact buttons. AI-built sites keep getting these wrong. The icon and the label sit on
one line, centred together vertically, with a set gap between them, and the icon is the same
size and the same colour as the label in every button. Every call and contact button is a solid
filled button with high contrast against whatever is behind it, sized for a thumb on a phone,
so it is the first thing the eye lands on in its section, never a pale outline or a plain text
link. Where the phone button and the Contact us button sit side by side, they are the same
height and the same width, and on a phone they stack full width.
- Cards and tiles. Every card in a row is the same height and the same width, however much text
each one carries. A link or button inside a card sits pinned to the bottom of the card, at the
same height in every card of the row, never floating straight after the last line of text.
Cards never blend into the background. A card on white gets a light tinted fill or a visible
border and the shadow, a card on a tinted band is white, and a card is never the same colour as
the section it sits on.
- Images. How photographs are cropped, the aspect ratios used, whether corners are rounded, and
the overlay used whenever text sits on a photograph so the text always reads.
- Movement. The scroll behaviour from section 5 above, written into the system so it applies to
every page.
2. THE HOME PAGE, FINISHED, SECTION BY SECTION
Build the whole home page in the first build. For every section, what it is called, its layout (one
column or two, which side the image sits, full width band or contained), the exact heading, the
copy word for word, the buttons and where each one goes. Never "a section about the services".
Write the section.
The home page carries at least these, in the order you judge best for this business: a hero, a
proof strip under it, the problems the business solves written as the customer feels them, the
services with prices where they exist, why this business and not the next one, the owner, what
happens on a first visit or first job, reviews, the areas served, a contact section with the form, and a
closing call to action. Read the home pages of the websites under THE STANDARD WE ARE AIMING AT
top to bottom before you build this one. Match the completeness of washingtonpachiro.com, the look
of sparksfamilychiropractic.com and the polish and movement of apple.com. Do not copy their
words.
3. THE HERO IS THE WHOLE FIRST IMPRESSION
- The main heading names the problem the business solves or the service they lead with, in the
customer's words. The line under it names the business and the town. Under those, the phone
number as a button and the Contact us button side by side, and under those one line of real,
checkable proof.
- If a real photograph of the owner, the team or the business exists, it carries the hero, with
the crop and the text side specified. If none exists, build a typography-led hero on a brand
colour band that looks finished on its own. A hero with a grey placeholder box in it is not
acceptable.
- On a phone the heading reads in two or three lines, and the phone button and the Contact us
button are on screen before anybody scrolls.
4. LAYOUT VARIETY, SO IT DOES NOT LOOK LIKE A TEMPLATE
- No two sections in a row share a layout. Image and text sections alternate sides. Full width
bands break up contained sections.
- Three equal cards with an icon on top appears once on a page at most.
- Not everything is centred. Section headings and body copy are left aligned unless a section is
a single short statement.
- No purple or blue gradients, no floating blobs, no glass effects, no abstract shapes, no
decorative squiggles, and no illustrations of people who do not exist.
5. DESIGNED FOR THE PHOTOGRAPHS THAT EXIST
Count the client's usable photographs before you design. If there are fewer than about six,
design a site that looks finished on colour, type and layout, and spend the photographs it has
where they count most. Placeholders are then few, small, and never in the hero. A site full of
placeholder boxes reads as unfinished however good the copy is.
6. THE HEADER, THE FOOTER AND THE MOBILE BUTTONS
Built in the first build exactly as sections 16 and 17 above describe. Every page built
afterwards reuses this header, this footer, these buttons and this design system without change.
Before you ship any page, check it against the mistakes AI-built sites make most often. Text
squeezed into a narrow column when nothing sits beside it. Call and contact buttons with the
icon and label out of line, buttons that do not stand out, and buttons that do nothing when
pressed, above all the circular mobile buttons. Mobile buttons that are not the same size as the
chat button or not level with it, and a blur behind them with a hard edge. Contact buttons that
land with the form cut off. Cards of uneven height, links that do not sit at
the bottom of their card, and cards the same colour as the background. Any table. Any "At a
glance" section or summary fact box.
LAST THING
Everything above describes a polished, premium, genuinely useful local business website that is
faster and clearer than the one it replaces, and that turns a visitor into an enquiry. Aim at
that, then build it.
